Codebook

**1. Set the scope**
- **What you label:** the unit (a changed span, or a whole output), what counts as "harmful", and what the rater sees (source and output, but not the condition or the probability).
- **What you don't label:** for example, harmless paraphrases and style preferences.
- **The rule for labelling:** one decision procedure, such as "ask whether a reader acting on the output would do something different from a reader acting on the source".

**2. Define each category with the same fields.** A table works:

| Field | What to write |
|---|---|
| Name | e.g. Wrong number/name/date |
| Definition | One sentence |
| Applies when | The concrete trigger |
| Does not apply when | The boundary with neighbouring categories |
| Example | A real example from your pilot, with source and output |
| Counter-example | Something that looks similar but doesn't count |

For example, "Wrong number" could use P03: the source says "no later than 09:00" and the output says "before noon". For a name, use P01: "Ms. Clara Jensen" became "Mr. Jensen". Real examples make the boundaries clear much faster than abstract definitions.

**3. Add decision rules for the hard cases**
- **Multiple errors in one span:** is it one label or several? Pick one rule, such as "one label per span, the most serious wins", and define the order.
- **Order of seriousness,** for example: reversed meaning, wrong number or name, critical omission, added information, then the milder ones.
- **What counts as critical omission:** define it by consequence (a deadline, a condition, a warning), not by length.
- **When to flag the item for discussion** instead of guessing.

**4. Add the procedure**
- How a rater works through an item, in order, and what to do when unsure.
- How long a pass should take, which you can time on your own pilot.
- A version number and date, because every change needs a record.

**How to develop it**
1. Draft it from your category list and the codes in Hayakawa and Wu & Arase.
2. Annotate 20–30 pilot outputs yourself. Every time you hesitate, write down why and add a rule.
3. Revise. Then label a new small set a few days later, and check whether you reproduce your own labels.
4. Freeze it **before** the real annotation, and give it to the second rater with a short tutorial. Wu & Arase used 2.5 hours.

A good test: if the second rater would have to ask you a question to apply it, the question belongs in the codebook.
